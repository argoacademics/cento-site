## The Weaver's Testament

_a specimen source text_

There was once a house at the edge of the water where the women wove through the winter and
unpicked their work each spring. They said a pattern held too long forgets what it was for.
Travellers thought this wasteful. The weavers thought the travellers had misunderstood the
nature of cloth, which is not a thing but a process wearing the appearance of a thing.

The eldest of them kept no pattern book. When asked how the designs survived from one
generation to the next, she said only that the cloth remembers the hand, and returned to her
frame. It was not evasion. She meant that the knowledge lived in the making and could not be
lifted out of it — that to write it down would be to keep the husk and lose the seed.

In the year the river changed course, a merchant came with an offer to buy every length the
house could produce. He spoke of markets in the south, of a remedial solution to the poverty
that had settled on the coast. The weavers listened. Then the eldest said that a loom worked
to another's rhythm produces a hamstrung hidebound thing, correct in every measure and dead in
the hand, and the offer was refused.

What the merchant could not see was that the refusal was itself the inheritance. Each
generation received not a technique but a posture toward the work: the willingness to unpick,
the suspicion of finished things, the long patience that treats an ending as a kind of failure. If the house had a creed, then
the very verdict of it was that nothing should be permitted to harden.

The last of the weavers left no cloth at all. She left a frame with the warp strung and the
weft not begun — the threads held tight and waiting, an invitation shaped like an unfinished
thing. Those who came after understood it as instruction. Some took up the shuttle. Some cut
the warp free and called the matter closed. Both, in their way, were answering the same
question, which the house had never once asked aloud and had spent four generations refusing
to settle.
